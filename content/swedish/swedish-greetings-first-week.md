Your first week in Sweden is full of small conversations: the bus driver, the neighbour in the stairwell, the cashier at ICA. The good news is that a handful of Swedish words carry you a long way. Here are the greetings Swedes really use, and the unwritten rules that come with them.

## Hello

- **Hej!** – Hi! / Hello! The one word you need. It works with everyone, from your boss to a shop assistant.
- **Hej hej!** – a friendly, slightly cheerful "hi".
- **God morgon!** – Good morning!
- **Tjena!** / **Tja!** – Hey! Casual, between friends and younger people.
- **Hallå?** – Hello? Used on the phone, or to get someone's attention.

## Goodbye

- **Hej då!** – Bye!
- **Vi ses!** – See you!
- **Ha det bra!** – Take care! (literally "have it good")
- **Ha en bra dag!** – Have a nice day!

Swedes often say *hej* when they leave as well: a short *hej hej!* on the way out is perfectly normal.

## Thank you, please and sorry

| Swedish | English | When |
|---|---|---|
| Tack! | Thanks! | All the time; also used for "please" when ordering: *En kaffe, tack.* |
| Tack så mycket! | Thank you very much! | When you really mean it |
| Varsågod! | Here you are! / You're welcome! | Handing something over, or answering *tack* |
| Ursäkta! | Excuse me! | To get past someone or get attention |
| Förlåt! | Sorry! | When you bump into someone or make a mistake |

Swedish has no everyday word for "please". Instead, Swedes are polite with **tack** and with questions: *Kan jag få…?* (Can I have…?)

## Introducing yourself

- **Jag heter Ana.** – My name is Ana.
- **Vad heter du?** – What is your name?
- **Jag kommer från Brasilien.** – I come from Brazil.
- **Var kommer du ifrån?** – Where are you from?
- **Trevligt att träffas!** – Nice to meet you!
- **Hur mår du?** – How are you?
- **Bra, tack! Och du?** – Good, thanks! And you?

## Du, not ni

In the 1960s and 70s Sweden went through the **du-reformen**: people stopped using the formal *ni* and titles like *herr* and *fru*. Today you say **du** (you) to almost everyone, including your doctor, your teacher and your manager, and first names are the norm. Using *ni* to one person can sound oddly stiff.

## Tack för senast

One phrase surprises many newcomers: **tack för senast**, "thanks for last time". When you meet someone you spent time with recently, at a dinner, a party or even a fika, Swedes thank you for it the *next* time you see each other. Answer *tack själv* (thank you too), and you will sound like a local.
